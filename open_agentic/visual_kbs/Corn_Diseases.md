### Anthracnose_leaf_spot_top_dieback
Across these images, corn leaves display discrete, oval to elongated lesions that are tan to brown at the center with darker reddish-brown to black borders, often surrounded by yellow to chlorotic halos that can expand and coalesce along leaf margins. The lesions vary in size from small pinpoint spots on younger tissue to larger, water-soaked or necrotic patches that merge and cause broad yellowing and marginal scorch, particularly evident on lower and emerging leaves. In more advanced cases, entire leaf tips or sections show a straw-colored, bleached-out necrosis with a distinct yellow transitional zone between healthy green tissue and the dead area, giving a "scorched" appearance. Some lesions show a characteristic spindle or diamond shape with a slightly sunken, dried texture and darkened central cores that may appear glassy or greasy when fresh. The disease progresses from older to younger leaves and is notably associated with proximity to infested crop debris on the soil surface, with early-stage plants showing concentrated lesion clusters near the base of emerging leaves.

### Anthracnose_stalk_rot
Across these images, the most striking feature is the shiny, jet-black to dark brown discoloration of the outer stalk tissue, particularly concentrated at and between the nodes, giving the lower stalk internodes a distinctly darkened, glazed appearance. In the internal cross-section view, the pith tissue is completely rotted away, leaving only the fibrous vascular strands intact in a hollow, stringy, straw-colored mass with scattered small black fungal structures (microsclerotia) visible as dark flecks within the degraded tissue. At the field scale, affected plants show premature whole-plant firing — leaves turning tan to brown from the bottom up while surrounding plants remain green — creating a scattered "dead plant" pattern within an otherwise healthy-looking canopy. The exterior stalk surface displays a characteristic shredded, bleached tan coloration with irregular dark blotching that is distinctly different from the uniform discoloration seen in other stalk rots. In the lodging image, the severe rotting of the crown and lower internodes has caused widespread stalk collapse at or near ground level, with plants falling over in a characteristic pattern indicating structural failure of the internal pith while the outer rind initially remained intact.

### Bacterial_stalk_rot
Affected corn plants exhibit a sudden, dramatic wilting and desiccation of leaves that progress from a water-soaked, pale green discoloration to a tan-to-brown papery dryness, often collapsing the entire above-ground canopy while surrounding plants remain green and healthy. The most diagnostically distinctive feature visible in the close-up image is a dark brown to black, macerated lesion at a stalk node, appearing water-soaked and soft with cracked, necrotic tissue that has a rotted, disintegrated texture concentrated at the internode junction. Affected plants show a characteristic "dead heart" or "fired" appearance — leaves bleach uniformly tan-brown from the inside out, often retaining their upright shape while completely desiccated, distinguishing this from fungal stalk rots where internal pith discoloration is more gradual. The stalk lesion appears greasy or slimy at the node in early stages, transitioning to a dry, shredded brown collapse, and the discoloration does not extend uniformly up the stalk but remains concentrated near the node. Individual plants die abruptly within a field of otherwise healthy plants, with the tassel often remaining intact atop the collapsed, brown foliage, and no powdery coatings, pustules, or discrete leaf spots are visible — the necrosis is diffuse and systemic rather than localized.

### Carbonum_leaf_spot
Lesions on leaves appear as oval to elongated necrotic spots with a tan or grayish-white center, surrounded by distinct concentric brown rings and a yellow halo, ranging from small circular spots (~5mm) to larger coalescing lesions several centimeters long. In more advanced cases, lesions expand along the leaf, developing a prominent yellow chlorotic zone around a darker brown necrotic core, sometimes causing tissue to split or tear longitudinally at the center. The distribution of lesions appears scattered across the leaf blade without strict vein-bounding, distinguishing it from diseases like gray leaf spot where lesions are strictly rectangular and vein-limited. On ears/cobs, a dense, dark grayish-black to sooty mold-like growth colonizes the tip and kernel area, causing kernel blackening and rotting concentrated at the tip end. Root and crown tissue shows progressive darkening and rotting, with a gradient visible across the four root specimens — from healthy white roots with pink-tinged crowns to severely blackened, necrotic, and shriveled root systems with near-complete crown rot.

### Charcoal_stalk_rot
The affected stalks show extensive internal disintegration, with the pith tissue shredded into loose, fibrous strands running longitudinally, leaving the interior largely hollow or collapsed. A defining visual feature is the presence of numerous tiny, discrete black specks (microsclerotia) densely scattered across the shredded internal pith tissue and visible on the inner stalk surfaces, particularly prominent in the lower images. The vascular bundles remain relatively intact as darker, thread-like strands running through the disintegrated pith, creating a characteristic stringy, rope-like internal appearance. The outer rind/cortex retains a straw-yellow to tan coloration and appears structurally intact compared to the severely degraded interior, though in the first image more advanced decay shows darker brown-to-black discoloration of the internal tissues alongside the black speckling. Across images, severity ranges from moderate fibrous shredding with scattered microsclerotia to near-complete pith destruction, but the consistent combination of longitudinal fiber separation, hollow internodes, and the distinctive pepper-like black sclerotial bodies embedded throughout the pith tissue is the most diagnostically distinguishing feature.

### Common_rust
Across these images, the disease manifests as small, oval to circular, raised pustules on corn leaf surfaces, ranging from orange-brown to reddish-brown in color, scattered individually or in loose clusters across the leaf blade. In early stages (images 1 and 3), pustules appear as discrete, slightly raised brick-red to cinnamon-brown bumps, relatively sparse and distributed randomly across the green leaf tissue, sometimes surrounded by faint yellow-orange halos. As severity increases (images 4 and 5), pustules become densely packed, rupturing the leaf epidermis to release powdery, rust-colored (urediniospore) masses, with the surrounding tissue turning necrotic, tan, or dark brown, and the leaf surface appearing visibly torn or shredded where pustules have erupted. The pustules are notably elongated-oval in shape, aligned somewhat parallel to the leaf veins, and in advanced cases (image 5) coalesce into large necrotic bands along the midrib and leaf surface, giving a dark, crusty, heavily eroded appearance. Unlike gray leaf spot lesions (which are rectangular and gray) or southern rust (which has smaller, more orange pustules concentrated on the upper leaf surface), these pustules appear on both leaf surfaces, are cinnamon to dark brown, and have a distinctly raised, eruptive texture.

### Common_smut
Across these images, the disease manifests as abnormal fleshy galls that form directly on corn tissue — including on the ear, stalk nodes, and individual kernels — replacing normal plant structures with grotesque, tumor-like swellings. In early stages, the galls appear as pale greenish-white to silver-white, smooth, and turgid masses with a firm, watery texture, often bulging irregularly from between leaf sheaths or along the cob (visible in images 3 and 4). As galls mature, the outer membrane ruptures to reveal a powdery, jet-black to dark brown mass of spores within, causing the entire gall to collapse into a dry, dusty, shredded structure with remnants of silvery-gray membrane still visible at the margins (visible in image 5). On the ear specifically, individual kernels become massively enlarged and deformed into rounded, lobed swellings that displace normal kernel rows, and in later infection the ear surface turns dark brown-black with visible spore masses (image 2). The galls vary considerably in size — from small pea-sized nodules to large fist-sized masses — and can occur on any above-ground meristematic tissue, with the distinctive progression from pale, smooth, and intact to dark, powdery, and ruptured being the key visual hallmark distinguishing this from other corn diseases.

### Corn_Stunt_Spiroplasma
Across these images, the most striking and consistent symptom is intense red-to-purple striping running parallel to the leaf veins, creating a bold streaked or banded pattern along the entire leaf blade — visible in both green, actively growing tissue and in dried, senesced material. In green tissue (images 3 and 5), the discoloration appears as alternating bands of dark maroon-red/purple and green, with the red pigmentation sometimes nearly overwhelming the green chlorophyll, and scattered small, pale cream-colored flecks or lesions interspersed along the stripes. The dried, mature tissue (images 1, 2, and 4) retains a distinctive pink-to-rose-purple cast over the straw-colored background, confirming the pigmentation persists through senescence and is visible at a distance across entire plants in the field. The striping appears to follow vascular tissue, suggesting systemic distribution throughout the leaf mesophyll alongside veins, and affects leaves across multiple canopy positions simultaneously. No powdery coatings, discrete circular lesions, or necrotic blighting are evident; instead the symptom is dominated entirely by this diffuse but vivid anthocyanin-based pigmentation arranged in a linear, vein-parallel pattern that distinguishes it from fungal leaf blights or mosaic virus patterns which typically show more irregular mottling or discrete spots.

### Crazy_top
Affected corn plants exhibit a striking proliferation of abnormal, strap-like or broom-like growth emerging from the upper stalk and ear zones, where numerous small, distorted, stiff leafy shoots arise in dense clusters rather than forming normal ear or tassel structures. The abnormal shoots are characteristically narrow, twisted, and compressed, producing a tight, frothy, or "witches' broom" mass of tissue that is distinctly different from healthy leaf architecture. Coloration of affected tissue ranges from pale yellow-green to normal green, sometimes with slight yellowing or browning at the margins and tips of the distorted shoots, while the surrounding healthy leaves maintain their normal dark green color. In earlier-stage plants (first image), excessive tillering or multiple-shoot emergence at the base is visible, with plants appearing overly leafy and compressed. The distorted masses lack any normal ear development — silk, husks, and kernels are replaced entirely by these leafy proliferations — and in more advanced cases (second image), the central growing point appears completely replaced by a dense, crinkled, dried mass of aborted or malformed tissue with darkened necrotic areas at the core of the proliferation.

### Diplodia_stalk_rot
The corn stalks shown are collapsed and lodged at or near the lower internodes, with the stems exhibiting a tan-to-brown discoloration and internal tissue breakdown that causes them to buckle rather than snap cleanly. On the outer stalk surface, small, discrete black pepper-like specks (pycnidia) are visible embedded in the rind tissue, particularly concentrated near the nodes and along internode surfaces, appearing as tiny raised or flush black dots scattered across the bleached, straw-colored stalk exterior. The internal pith appears shredded, disintegrated, and darkened brown, leaving a hollow or fibrous cavity where healthy white pith would normally be present. The nodes themselves show dark brown to nearly black discoloration, with rotted leaf sheaths clinging to the base of the stalk displaying similar dark streaking and browning. The overall plant appearance is one of premature senescence — completely desiccated, bleached gray-tan foliage lying flattened on the dark soil — with the distinguishing feature being the presence of those black pycnidia embedded in the rind of otherwise straw-colored, externally intact-looking stalk tissue, differentiating this from Gibberella stalk rot (which shows pink/red internal discoloration) or charcoal rot (which shows gray powdery internal discoloration).

### Downy_mildew
Affected corn plants display a striking yellowing and pale green discoloration concentrated in the whorl and upper emerging leaves, often presenting as a diffuse, washed-out chlorosis rather than discrete lesions. The yellowed tissue frequently shows a distinctive light-green to yellow-white striping or banding pattern running parallel to the leaf veins, giving leaves a streaked appearance. Severely affected plants exhibit excessive leaf curling, twisting, and crinkling — particularly in the youngest central leaves — causing the whorl to appear bunched, distorted, and unable to unfurl normally. In the close-up image, affected leaf tissue shows a wrinkled, corrugated texture with pale yellow-green coloration around the midrib and leaf base, contrasting sharply with the darker green of healthy tissue on the same plant. At the field level, symptomatic plants are noticeably stunted compared to neighboring healthy plants, standing shorter with a more compact, rosette-like appearance, and the overall plant color is a lighter, more yellow-green tone that stands out distinctly within the otherwise uniformly dark green canopy.

### Ear_rots_Aspergillus
Across these images, the most consistent and distinguishing visual feature is a **olive-green to yellow-green powdery or dusty mold growth** coating the kernels, ranging from small localized patches (image 1) to extensive coverage across large portions of the ear (images 5 and 6). The affected kernels beneath the mold appear discolored — shrunken, darkened, or bleached — with a dull, sunken surface texture compared to the plump, glossy appearance of healthy adjacent kernels. In more advanced cases (images 2 and 3), additional secondary molds appear simultaneously, including white mycelial growth and dark/black fungal masses, suggesting mixed infections, though the characteristic dusty olive-green powder remains the primary identifier. The distribution pattern tends to follow rows or bands across the ear surface, often initiating at the silk end or mid-ear and spreading outward, with sharp transitions between healthy yellow kernels and diseased areas. Image 4 shows the soil/root zone context with a mix of olive-green and white granular masses, likely representing sclerotia or conidial masses present in the growing medium. The powdery, dusty texture of the green-yellow sporulation — as opposed to the cottony or felty texture typical of *Fusarium* or *Diplodia* — is the key visual differentiator from other ear rots.

### Ear_rots_Diplodia
Affected ears display a dense, white to grayish-white cottony mycelial growth that begins at the base of the ear and progresses toward the tip, often binding the husk leaves tightly to the cob with a bleached, straw-colored discoloration. The kernels become shrunken, dull, and tan to light brown in color, losing their normal waxy sheen and appearing chalky or powdery, with the mycelium visibly filling the spaces between kernel rows. In close-up views, tiny black pycnidia (pepper-like specks) are scattered across the kernel surface and husk tissue, which is a highly distinguishing feature. The husk leaves themselves turn pale, papery, and bleached prematurely compared to surrounding healthy plant tissue, and in field views the entire ear appears desiccated and whitened well before normal maturity. Across the images, severity ranges from partial ear involvement with localized basal rotting and white mycelium to near-complete ear colonization with fully shriveled, discolored kernels, while the stalk near the ear attachment often shows reddish-brown streaking or darkening.

### Ear_rots_Gibberella
Across these images, the most consistent and distinguishing visual feature is a **pink-to-reddish-purple mold growth** colonizing corn ears, husks, and stalk sheaths, often appearing as a dense, cottony-to-powdery fungal mycelium that starts at the silk/tip end of the ear and progresses downward toward the base. On husks and leaf sheaths, the infection manifests as brown-to-reddish-tan water-soaked discoloration streaked with dark black sooty masses of fungal spores (perithecia), visible as scattered black specks or smeared streaks along the vascular tissue. The infected ear kernels appear shrunken, discolored reddish-brown to dark purple, and heavily colonized with white-to-pink mycelium matting between and over individual kernels, with tip rot being the predominant pattern in early-to-moderate infections while severe cases show near-complete ear colonization. On stalk internodes, pinkish-white mycelial growth is visible under the leaf sheath alongside characteristic dark black stromatic tissue. The husks in advanced infections take on a bleached, tan-gray, papery texture with embedded black perithecia, and the overall severity ranges from partial tip colonization to complete ear mummification, all consistently showing that diagnostic reddish-pink fungal pigmentation.

### Ear_rots_Trichoderma
The affected corn ears display a distinctive blue-green to teal-colored powdery or granular fungal growth concentrated in the spaces between kernels, along kernel crowns, and across kernel surfaces, creating a characteristic mottled or patchy discoloration pattern against the yellow-orange kernel background. The mold growth ranges from light blue-green flecking (earlier/milder infection) to dense, heavily coated coverage where kernels appear almost entirely obscured by the dark blue-green mycelium and spore masses. Infected kernels themselves show surface bleaching, pale yellowing, or tan-to-salmon discoloration beneath the fungal growth, and in more advanced cases kernels appear shrunken, shriveled, or collapsed. The blue-green coloration is notably concentrated between kernel rows and at kernel bases rather than uniformly coating the entire surface, giving a striped or banded appearance when viewed from a distance. The husks and silks in severely affected ears also show greenish-black spotting and discoloration, consistent with fungal spread beyond the kernels. This distinctive blue-green powdery coloration — as opposed to the pink/red of Fusarium, white cottony growth of some other molds, or olive/black of Cladosporium — is the key visual differentiator.

### Eyespot
Across these images, the affected maize leaves display numerous small, circular to oval lesions scattered densely across the leaf blade, each with a distinctive "eye-like" appearance — a reddish-brown to orange central spot surrounded by a bright yellow-orange halo, giving a bullseye or target-like pattern. In early stages (images 1–2), the lesions appear as vivid yellow-orange rings with a defined reddish-brown center point, still isolated and relatively uniform in size (approximately 2–5mm diameter). As severity progresses (images 3 and 5), the centers darken to deep reddish-brown or necrotic tan, the yellow halos become less distinct, and lesions begin to coalesce, leading to generalized yellowing and necrosis of large leaf areas, particularly toward the margins and tips. The lesions appear to follow the parallel venation of maize leaves, often aligning in loose rows between veins. The field-level view (image 4) shows that heavily infected plants exhibit a characteristic bleached, stippled appearance visible from a distance, with widespread yellowing across the canopy. Notably, the lesions lack the elongated rectangular shape of Gray Leaf Spot or the cigar-shaped necrosis of Northern Corn Leaf Blight, remaining distinctly round with their concentric color zonation as the key differentiating feature.

### Fusarium
Across these images, the most consistent and distinguishing visual feature is the presence of white-to-pinkish cottony or powdery mycelial growth appearing between and around individual kernels, forming irregular, patchy clusters rather than a uniform coating. On yellow-kerneled ears, this white mold is strikingly visible as discrete tufts or streaks running along kernel rows, sometimes associated with kernel discoloration ranging from orange-pink to brown where tissue is decaying. The second image reveals internal damage with a visible larval tunnel (likely from an insect entry wound that facilitated infection), surrounded by white mycelial mat and degraded, sunken kernels — illustrating that infection often follows mechanical injury. The split stalk image shows internal browning and shredding of the pith tissue, with vascular bundles becoming stringy and discolored tan-to-brown, indicating systemic stalk rot. On the white-kerneled ear, a subtle pinkish-lavender discoloration appears at kernel crowns and between rows — a more understated but diagnostically important sign on lighter-colored grain. Overall, the white-to-salmon/pink mycelial growth concentrated in discrete patches on the ear, combined with internal stalk tissue disintegration, are the hallmark visual indicators distinguishing this disease from other kernel rots.

### Goss_wilt
Affected corn plants display long, water-soaked to tan or brown necrotic streaks running parallel to leaf veins, often spanning much of the leaf blade, with irregular wavy margins rather than clean borders. A highly distinctive feature visible in the close-up leaf images is the presence of small, dark, glistening "freckles" — tiny water-soaked or dark green to black bacterial exudate dots scattered within or adjacent to the necrotic lesions, appearing as if flecked onto the leaf surface. On some leaves, a pinkish-to-reddish discoloration is visible along the midrib or lesion margins, and the necrotic tissue can appear grayish-white in the center with a yellow-green halo transitioning to the healthy tissue. At the stalk level, cross-sections show a white, mold-like bacterial slime or ooze colonizing the internal pith tissue. At the field scale, severely affected plants show rapid, complete leaf firing and desiccation — entire plants turning brown and dead while adjacent rows or hybrids remain green — creating a striking contrast that suggests varietal susceptibility differences and rapid systemic spread.

### Gray_leaf_spot
Lesions appear as rectangular to elongated necrotic streaks that run parallel between leaf veins, giving them a distinctly linear, stripe-like shape constrained by the vascular tissue. Early-stage lesions (images 1 and 3) show small, tan-to-pale gray centers with water-soaked or yellow-green halos and occasional reddish-brown borders, while advanced lesions (images 1 and 4) coalesce into larger gray-tan patches with a bleached, papery texture. In severe infections (images 2 and 5), lesions merge extensively across the entire leaf surface, producing a blighted, straw-colored to light brown appearance that can affect whole leaves, with the tissue looking dry and sometimes slightly silvery-gray under reflected light. The lesions lack the concentric banding or broad yellow halos typical of other blights, and their strict parallel orientation between veins is a distinguishing hallmark. The final image shows a fully senescent leaf where confluent lesions have rendered the tissue uniformly tan-brown with darker streak remnants still visible, representing the terminal disease stage.

### Head_smut
Across these images, the disease manifests primarily at the ear/tassel structures of corn, replacing normal kernel and floral tissue with distinctive gall-like masses that progress through clearly visible stages. In early stages (images 1 and 5), individual kernels are replaced by swollen, pale blue-gray to silver-white ovoid structures nestled within darkened, thread-like remnants of silk, giving a clustered, bead-like appearance with smooth, waxy surfaces. As the disease advances, these galls rupture to expose masses of dark olive-brown to jet-black powdery spore material (teliospores), visible in images 3 and 4, where entire ear structures are converted into large, irregular black sooty masses that may exceed the size of a normal ear. In the dried/late-stage images (2 and 4), the husks split open to reveal collapsed, dark brown-to-black shredded tissue with a fibrous, crumbling texture, sometimes accompanied by secondary sprouting of plant tissue through the decayed mass. A consistent distinguishing feature is the complete replacement of reproductive structures (kernels/florets) rather than surface lesions on leaves or stems, with the black powdery spore masses being the definitive identifying characteristic.

### Holcus_spot
Lesions appear as small, discrete, circular to slightly oval spots with a pale tan or white necrotic center, surrounded by a distinct reddish-brown to orange-brown border, giving them a clearly defined "bull's-eye" or "halo" appearance against the green leaf tissue. Individual spots are relatively small — roughly 5–15 mm in diameter — and are scattered across the leaf blade without strong tendency to follow leaf venation. In early stages (closeup images), lesions are few and isolated, with crisp circular margins and a bleached, papery center; in more advanced cases (the pressed specimen images), numerous lesions have coalesced into large, irregular necrotic patches covering substantial portions of the leaf, with surrounding tissue showing brown discoloration and some water-soaked appearance. The necrotic tissue in mature lesions appears dry and bleached (cream to white), distinctly sunken or papery in texture, with no visible fungal sporulation, powdery coating, or mycelial growth on the surface. The lesions are distributed randomly across the lamina rather than being confined to leaf edges or midrib, and the healthy green tissue between early-stage spots remains unaffected, making individual lesions stand out sharply.

### Maize_chlorotic_dwarf_virus
Affected maize plants display a pronounced interveinal chlorosis with irregular pale yellow to light green streaking running parallel to the leaf veins, creating a distinctive striped or mottled pattern visible most clearly on younger, emerging leaves. The chlorotic streaking varies in intensity — some leaves show fine, feathery yellow lines against a darker green background (as seen in the close-up leaf image), while more severely affected plants exhibit broad yellowing or bleaching of entire leaf sections. Severely symptomatic plants show significant stunting, with a rosette-like or bushy appearance caused by shortened internodes, producing a compressed, flattened growth habit with leaves emerging nearly horizontally rather than at normal angles. Leaf margins and tips of heavily affected plants show necrotic browning and tissue death, and the overall plant architecture appears collapsed or sprawling at the base. The youngest leaves in the whorl frequently appear pale, washed-out, or nearly white-green compared to older tissue, and some plants show excessive tillering (multiple side shoots at the base), a hallmark symptom distinguishable from simple nutrient deficiency by the combination of stunting, tillering, and the systemic, vein-parallel chlorotic streaking pattern.

### Maize_dwarf_mosaic_virus
Across these images, the most consistent and striking symptom is a mosaic or streaking pattern of pale yellow-green to cream-white discoloration running **parallel to the leaf veins**, creating irregular longitudinal stripes and streaks against the darker green background tissue. The chlorotic streaks vary in width from fine pinstripes to broad bands occupying nearly half the leaf blade, and in more advanced cases (images 4 and 5), entire interveinal zones become bleached tan-to-straw colored with necrotic tissue, suggesting progressive tissue death following initial chlorosis. Early-stage symptoms (images 1 and 3) show a subtle, diffuse mottling with soft yellow-green patches concentrated near the midrib, while later stages display sharply contrasting pale streaks distributed across the full leaf width. The leaf margins and overall leaf shape appear largely intact — there is no significant curling, wilting, or distortion visible — distinguishing this from nutrient deficiencies (which tend toward uniform marginal yellowing) or physical damage. A key distinguishing feature is the **fine, broken, discontinuous nature** of the pale streaks rather than solid uniform bands, giving the leaf a characteristic "painted" or brushed appearance that follows vascular tissue precisely.

### Maize_streak_virus
Across these images, the most prominent visual feature is a striking chlorotic striping pattern running parallel to the leaf veins, creating alternating yellow-white and green streaks along the entire leaf blade length. In close-up views (particularly image 4), these streaks appear as fine, discontinuous dashes and flecks of pale yellow to white coloration interspersed with darker green tissue, giving leaves a distinctly mottled, streaked appearance rather than solid yellowing. Severely affected plants show near-complete chlorosis with leaves appearing pale whitish-green and wilted, with a characteristic drooping and twisting of the leaf lamina, while less affected plants in the same field display milder streaking against otherwise green tissue. The field-level views reveal uneven disease distribution with heavily symptomatic stunted plants interspersed among taller, healthier-looking plants, and infected plants appear notably shorter with reduced vigor. Notably, there are no discrete necrotic lesions, spots, or powdery coatings visible — the symptom is exclusively a vein-parallel streaking chlorosis that varies in intensity from light speckled flecking to near-complete leaf bleaching depending on severity.

### Northern_corn_leaf_blight
The lesions appear as elongated, spindle- or elliptical-shaped necrotic spots that run parallel to the leaf veins, ranging from roughly 2–15 cm in length, with a distinctly cigar- or canoe-like outline. Coloration progresses from tan/grayish-brown in the center to darker brown or reddish-brown borders, and in more advanced lesions (second and fifth images) the center becomes pale straw or bleached-white with prominent dark brown banding running longitudinally within the lesion. The lesion margins are relatively diffuse rather than sharply defined, blending gradually into surrounding green tissue, and the long axis of each lesion is strictly parallel to the leaf veins, never crossing them. In the third image, a grayish, slightly powdery or silvery surface sheen is visible on the lesion surface, consistent with sporulation of the pathogen. Lesions are distributed across the leaf blade and can occur singly or as multiple discrete spots on the same leaf, with no obvious restriction to leaf margins or midrib, and small secondary orange-yellow fleck lesions (likely rust pustules) are visible in the background of several images, distinguishing those as co-infections rather than part of the primary lesion morphology.

### Physoderma_brown_spot
Across these images, the disease presents as small, yellow to pale-green flecks or spots densely scattered across the leaf blade in a stippled or speckled pattern, often giving the leaf a mottled, washed-out appearance against the darker green background. On the midrib and leaf sheath, distinctive dark brown to black, roughly circular to elongated lesions appear — these are notably more discrete and darker than the diffuse leaf blade flecking, and in several images they align in a linear series along the midrib. ### Physoderma_stalk_rot
The most striking visual feature across these images is a distinct, dark brown to black rotting lesion at or near the crown/lower internode of the corn stalk, appearing as a sharply demarcated necrotic ring or band that encircles the stalk at a node, often creating an almost "pinched" or constricted appearance where the rotted tissue separates the upper and lower stalk segments. The first field image also shows a completely lodged plant being held by a researcher, illustrating that the structural failure occurs specifically at the rotted node, causing the stalk to snap or fold cleanly at that point rather than showing generalized wilting or collapse.

### Southern_Corn_Leaf_Blight
Lesions on corn leaves appear as elongated, elliptical to rectangular tan or straw-colored spots with distinct, relatively straight parallel edges that run between leaf veins, giving them a characteristic "cigar-shaped" or oblong appearance constrained by the vascular tissue. Early-stage lesions (visible in images 2 and 5) are small, water-soaked to light brown oval spots, while advanced infections (image 1) show numerous coalescing tan-gray lesions with necrotic centers and slightly darker brown borders scattered across the entire leaf surface, sometimes accompanied by small yellow-orange rust-like flecks nearby. The lesions have a dry, papery texture and bleached straw-tan coloration at their centers, with no powdery or fuzzy surface coating visible. Image 3 shows a seedling progression series with progressive stunting, reddish-pink discoloration of lower stems, and rotted seed/seedling bases alongside an ear showing kernel abortion and partial fill — indicating the pathogen also causes seedling blight and ear rot with missing kernel rows on an otherwise intact cob.

### Southern_rust
Across these images, the primary symptom is the presence of numerous small, circular to slightly oval pustules scattered densely across the upper leaf surface of corn, appearing as bright orange to brick-red or rusty-brown raised dots, typically 0.5–1mm in diameter. The pustules are notably concentrated and clustered, often appearing in dense patches or bands running parallel to the leaf veins, and in early stages (images 1, 2, 5) they display a vivid orange-red color with a faint yellow halo surrounding each pustule. A key distinguishing visual feature is that the pustules appear predominantly on the upper leaf surface and are very densely packed compared to common rust, with a more vivid, almost golden-orange coloration rather than a darker cinnamon-brown. In more advanced stages (images 3, 4), pustules darken to a deeper reddish-brown and surrounding leaf tissue begins to show extensive chlorosis, necrosis, and tan papery collapse, with pustules sometimes rupturing to release powdery spore masses. The first image also shows white elongated insect casts (likely aphid exuviae) scattered among the pustules, which are incidental, while the overall pustule distribution remains distinctly scattered-to-clustered across the entire leaf blade surface rather than confined to lesion margins.

### Stewarts_wilt
Across these images, young corn seedlings display irregular, pale to yellow-green streaking and striping running longitudinally along the leaf blades, with affected tissue appearing water-soaked or bleached in contrast to the darker healthy green tissue. Some leaves show marginal scorching or browning at the tips and edges, with a dried, necrotic appearance particularly evident in the lower, older leaves. Notably, some plants show more severe wilting and collapse of lower leaves while newer growth may still appear relatively intact, suggesting a systemic progression from the base upward.

### Tar_spot
The most striking and consistent feature across all images is the presence of small, discrete, raised black stromata (fungal fruiting bodies) scattered across the leaf surface, ranging from pinpoint-sized dots to slightly larger irregular black specks approximately 1–3 mm in diameter, which appear glossy and slightly embossed — distinctly different from flat discoloration. In early-stage images, these black structures appear on otherwise green leaf tissue, sometimes surrounded by small chlorotic (yellow-tan) halos or necrotic borders that give a "target-like" micro-appearance. As severity progresses, the surrounding tissue transitions from green to tan, orange-brown, and finally dark brown, with the black pycnidia/stromata becoming even more densely packed and the entire leaf blade becoming necrotic and papery. The second image notably shows elongated, rectangular tan lesions with dark borders running parallel to the leaf veins, which represent a slightly different lesion morphology but still bear the characteristic dark fungal bodies. A distinguishing feature visible in multiple images is a purplish-red discoloration of the midrib, particularly prominent in images 2 and 5, suggesting vascular involvement at higher disease severity. The distribution of the black stromata is scattered and non-confluent in early stages but becomes overwhelmingly dense in advanced stages, covering nearly the entire leaf surface with no clear zonal banding pattern as would be seen in other foliar diseases.
