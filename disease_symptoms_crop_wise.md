## Crop: Alfalfa Diseases

### Verticillium Wilt

**Symptoms:**
In Alfalfa, Verticillium wilt manifests primarily as a distinct, progressive yellowing and wilting of older leaves, coupled with interveinal chlorosis that results in a distinct mottled pattern across the leaf blades. Affected tissues display a soft, wilted texture, while the stem may exhibit longitudinal cankers with a dark, sunken appearance, sometimes accompanied by a slight splitting. Symptoms can appear in patches, often starting at the base and progressing upward, affecting both leaves and stems. The discolouration is typically a pale yellow to brown, with the margins of the wilting leaves remaining relatively intact, thus differentiating it from other leaf diseases. There are no notable halos or spore structures present; however, underlying vascular tissue may show a dark, stained appearance upon dissection. Unlike Phytophthora root rot, which tends to cause a rapid collapse and blackened roots, Verticillium wilt progresses more slowly and is characterized by a systemic pattern of leaf yellowing without extensive root necrosis.

### Anthracnose Leaf Spot Top Dieback

**Symptoms:**
Anthracnose leaf spot top dieback in corn is characterized by distinct oval to irregularly shaped lesions that appear as dark brown to black spots on the upper leaves. These spots can expand significantly, often leading to tissue necrosis and curling of the leaf margins, creating a wilting effect in the upper canopy. The lesions typically have a raised texture and are surrounded by a yellow halo, indicating the plant's response to the infection. In severe cases, the black lesions can coalesce and cause dieback of the leaf tips, resulting in a characteristic top-down browning. The affected tissues exhibit a tissue texture that ranges from slightly sunken to necrotic and dry. The distribution of these symptoms is commonly observed first on older leaves before progressing to younger foliage. Unlike Gray Leaf Spot, which features elongated lesions without halos, Anthracnose leaf spot prominently displays raised lesions with distinct yellow margins, aiding in its identification.

### Anthracnose Stalk Rot

**Symptoms:**
Anthracnose stalk rot on corn is characterized by the presence of elongated, dark brown to black lesions that typically form on the lower stems and leaf sheaths; these lesions may appear sunken with slightly raised, irregular margins. The lesions vary in size but often reach lengths of several inches, leading to a distinctive pattern of necrosis that can encircle the stem, causing wilting and discoloration. Affected tissue may show a watery, soft rot with cream-colored spore masses or exudate pooling in the affected areas. Occasionally, you may observe dark brown gummosis at the base of stems where the tissue integrity is severely compromised. The spread of these symptoms is often banded or patchy, with a predominance around the nodes and near the base of the plant. One key difference between Anthracnose and Fusarium stalk rot, a visually similar disease, is that Anthracnose lesions generally exhibit a more defined sunken appearance with raised margins, while Fusarium tends to cause a more uniform, shallow discoloration.

This disease primarily affects the stalk, but can also impact the lower leaves adjacent to the stem. A key difference between bacterial stalk rot and southern corn leaf blight is that the latter typically presents with distinct, elongated lesions along the leaf margins rather than the soft, dark, and exudative spots predominantly found on the stalk.

Infected areas show no clear halos or defined margins, but spores may burst out from the galls, creating clusters of powdery black spores at maturity. Importantly, unlike other diseases such as ear rot, common smut specifically targets ear formation, leading to a distinctive swelling rather than the rotting and softening of kernels.

### Goss Wilt

**Symptoms:**
Goss wilt in corn (Zea mays) manifests primarily through distinctive wilting and yellowing of leaves, often starting at the base and progressing upward. Notably, Goss wilt uniquely affects both the vascular system and the foliage, leading to a characteristic gradual loss of turgor pressure in the entire plant, eventually causing whole plant wilting. This distinct pattern of rapid wilting and vascular discoloration sets Goss wilt apart from Northern Corn Leaf Blight, which presents more with elongated lesions rather than the wilting and vascular symptoms seen in Goss wilt.

A key difference between powdery mildew and downy mildew—another disease of mango—is that powdery mildew lacks the dark, fuzzy growth associated with the latter and instead presents a fine, dry mycelium that does not adhere to the leaf surface.

### Sooty Mould

### Brown Stem Rot

**Symptoms:**
Brown Stem Rot (Cadophora gregata) presents characteristic foliar symptoms, including chlorosis and necrosis between leaf veins, followed by leaf curling and death. Leaf symptoms can vary based on the soybean variety, fungal strain, and environmental conditions. When stems are split, internal browning of vascular tissue and pith is evident, especially at nodes and in the lower stem, though externally, infected stems may look healthy. This disease is more severe when temperatures are cool and soil moisture is adequate. The pathogen can reproduce on soybean residue throughout the winter. An important factor to note is that the condition's severity is heightened in soils with low phosphorus and potassium and a soil pH below 6.5.

Compared to Sudden Death Syndrome, Brown Stem Rot is differentiated by the presence of internal browning in the pith.

### Diaporthe

### Soybean Cyst Nematode

**Symptoms:**
Soybean Cyst Nematode (SCN) infection manifests primarily at the root level, where eggs and juveniles create distinctive galls that appear as small, round, pale-yellow to white structures, often visible as raised bumps on the root surface. These galls vary in size from 1 to 3 mm in diameter and are distributed along fibrous root systems, leading to stunted growth and wilting of the aboveground plant, which can further exhibit yellowing or chlorosis of leaves. In contrast to foliar diseases, the root galls lack any coatings or halos but can be surrounded by brownish necrotic tissue if secondary infections occur. Unlike other diseases, such as Phytophthora root rot, SCN galls are more physically prominent, raised, and bulbous, rather than the sunken and mushy lesions typically seen in root rot infections. The visual identification of SCN relies on recognizing the characteristic root galls, their specific pale coloration, and their distinct elevated texture compared to other root-related diseases in soybeans.

### Soybean Dwarf Mosaic Virus

**Symptoms:**
Soybean Dwarf Mosaic Virus manifests prominently on the leaves of infected soybean plants, characterized by distinctive mottling and mosaic patterns that encompass irregular, light green to yellowish areas interspersed with dark green patches. The affected tissue appears slightly curled and crinkled, creating a rough texture, while the overall leaf has a stunted, dwarfed appearance. These mottled areas often display sunken or superficial characteristics, lacking raised lesions or galls. The symptoms are symmetrically distributed, usually present on both upper and lower leaf surfaces, often causing yellowing and wilting of new foliage. Additionally, the leaf margins may show subtle necrotic streaks, though no spore structures or exudates are present. Unlike many other viral infections that create defined necrotic spots, Soybean Dwarf Mosaic Virus is differentiated by its mosaic coloration without prominent wilting of the stems or leaf drop, which are common features in other diseases like Soybean Cyst Nematode.

### Soybean Vein Necrosis Virus

**Symptoms:**
Symptoms caused by Soybean vein necrosis virus (SVNV) often begin as yellowing near leaf veins that develops into reddish-brown lesions, causing death of leaf tissue. Lesions follow or spread from leaf veins. Leaf veins may limit lesions, resulting in an angular lesion appearance. Clear, yellow, or dark brown discoloration of leaf veins can occur. Browning of veins may be especially noticeable on the leaf underside. The virus is vectored by soybean thrips and possibly other thrips, and hot, dry weather that favors thrips may cause increased disease spread. Weeds may provide an overwintering host for SVNV; thrips migrating from the south may also carry the virus. 

The key difference is that soybean vein necrosis can be differentiated from similar diseases by the specific angular appearance of lesions following the leaf veins.

### Soybean Rust

**Symptoms:**
Symptoms of soybean rust begin on lower leaves after flowering, starting as small, gray spots that change to tan or reddish-brown. These lesions form on the lower leaf surfaces as scattered spots that may appear translucent when held up to the sun. Mature lesions can contain one to many small pustules, predominantly found on lower leaf surfaces. The disease is most commonly observed in cool, wet weather or high humidity, conditions that favor the spread of the lesions. This disease can cause significant yield loss if conditions allow for its spread within fields. 

A key difference from bacterial blight is that soybean rust lesions are often found beneath the leaves with a pustule formation, whereas bacterial blight lesions are primarily reddish-brown with a yellow halo and can cause leaf tattering.

### Stem Canker

**Symptoms:**
Stem canker onsoybeans presents as sunken lesions that develop on the stems, characterized by dark brown to black discoloration, often with a distinctive water-soaked appearance around the edges. These lesions can be irregularly shaped, typically ranging from 1 to 5 centimeters in size, and may coalesce, leading to larger areas of necrosis. The affected tissue may display a rough texture as it deteriorates, occasionally surrounded by lighter-colored halos that indicate a secondary infection. In advanced stages, these cankers may exude a gummy secretion and present with raised edges, giving a pronounced appearance against the otherwise healthy green stem. Symptoms can also extend into the base of the stem, with the cankers potentially causing wilting and stunted growth if left unchecked. While both stem canker and Phytophthora rot show stem discoloration, stem canker is distinguished by its sunken lesions and gummosis, whereas Phytophthora rot typically leads to a more uniform, soft rot without the distinct lesion formation.

### Sudden Death Syndrome

**Symptoms:**
Foliar symptoms of sudden death syndrome (SDS) are a result of a toxin produced by the fungus, moving from roots to the leaves. Initial symptoms rarely appear until after flowering, showing scattered yellow spots between leaf veins. These spots grow to form large chlorotic and necrotic blotches between the leaf veins, while the midvein and major lateral veins remain green. Leaflets eventually drop, but petioles remain on the stem. Diseased plants also exhibit rotted taproots and lateral roots. When stems are cut lengthwise, the woody tissue of the taproot is discolored light gray to brown, with discoloration potentially extending up to two inches above ground. A distinctive feature for diagnosing SDS is the presence of white pith in SDS-infected plants, contrasting with brown discoloration of the pith found in brown stem rot (BSR). 

Sudden death syndrome is visually distinguishable from brown stem rot by the internal stem discoloration; SDS-infected plants have white pith, while BSR shows brown discoloration.

### Tobacco Streak Virus

**Symptoms:**
In soybean plants infected with Tobacco Streak Virus, characteristic symptoms begin with small, irregularly shaped, light green to yellow mottled areas on leaves, often appearing as scattered spots. These spots can expand into distinct necrotic lesions, typically oval or elongated, and are often surrounded by a yellow halo that highlights their edges. Affected tissues may exhibit a slightly leathery texture, and the lesions appear as sunken areas, giving the leaves a dappled look. The upper leaf surface may show these symptoms, while the undersides may remain mostly unaffected, making the lesions visually prominent. Additionally, lower leaves may display a more pronounced wilting or curling, indicating systemic impact. The distribution of lesions tends to be random across the leaf, with some leaves exhibiting multiple spots, while younger leaves are often less affected. Unlike similar diseases such as Soybean Mosaic Virus, which presents with mottling and distortion primarily in younger leaves, Tobacco Streak Virus primarily manifests distinct, scattered necrotic spots on older foliage without significant leaf curling or overall distortion.

### Top Dieback

**Symptoms:**
Top dieback in soybeans is characterized by the progressive wilting and die-off of terminal shoots, resulting in stunted growth and a discoloured appearance of affected leaves, which present a mosaic of yellowing to brown patches. The leaves exhibit distinct necrotic lesions that are often sunken and irregularly shaped, appearing as dark brown to black spots, typically with a yellow halo surrounding them. These lesions may also display a fuzzy texture due to the presence of mycelium as the disease progresses, particularly on leaf undersides. The topmost leaves may curl and become brittle, while the stems can show signs of dark cankers, leading to a further decline in vitality and potential lodging. In severe cases, there may be a noticeable presence of exudate or gummosis at affected sites. Symptoms primarily manifest on the upper leaves and stems, while pods and seeds are generally spared but can be indirectly affected due to overall plant stress. A key difference between top dieback and other soybean diseases like Phytophthora root rot is that top dieback primarily affects the upper plant parts, whereas Phytophthora shows more pronounced root and lower stem symptoms.

### White Mold

**Symptoms:**
White mold is primarily recognized by fluffy, white fungal growth on soybean stems. Initial symptoms typically develop from R3 to R6 as gray to white lesions appear at the nodes. These lesions tend to rapidly progress above and below the nodes, sometimes girdling the stem. As the disease advances, white, fluffy mold can cover the infected areas, especially during periods of high humidity. Initially, affected soybean plants exhibit leaf tissues between major veins turning a gray-green cast; eventually, leaves die and turn completely brown while remaining attached to the stem. A characteristic sign includes the eventual visibility of black sclerotia embedded within the mold on stem lesions. The fungus survives in the soil for several years as sclerotia, beginning the disease cycle afresh when conditions are conducive to growth. 

White mold can be confounded with other diseases like the Diaporthe complex and sudden death syndrome due to overlapping symptoms.

## Crop: Wheat Diseases

### Rust

**Symptoms:**
Rust on wheat is characterized by the appearance of distinctive rust-colored pustules that emerge on the surface of leaves and stems, exhibiting a raised texture akin to small blisters. These pustules, typically round or oval, vary in size from 1 to 5 mm and often form in clusters, giving a speckled appearance to the affected areas. The infected tissues display a yellowing or chlorosis around the pustules, with some lesions exhibiting dark, defined margins. In severe cases, the leaves may curl or die back, showing signs of wilting, while the rust spores may produce a dusty orange-red coating on the surface of the plant. Unlike other diseases such as powdery mildew, which presents as a white, superficial growth with a velvety texture, rust is marked by its distinct, raised pustules and the dark halos that may form around the lesions, highlighting its unique visual footprint for accurate identification.
