## Crop: Alfalfa Diseases

### Verticillium Wilt

**Symptoms:**
Verticillium wilt in alfalfa presents as a distinctive pattern of wilting, primarily affecting the leaves and stems. In the early stages, symptoms may include yellowing and a characteristic dark green discoloration along the leaf margins, gradually progressing to necrotic lesions that are sunken, with a central tan area. The affected tissue often appears shriveled and dry, with leaves exhibiting a curling or drooping shape. As the disease advances, the stem may develop a canker-like appearance with dark streaks, while some plants show a striking chlorosis that can be unevenly distributed, leading to patches of green intermixed with yellowing foliage. A key visual feature of Verticillium wilt is the absence of any raised structures or spore formations, which sets it apart from similar conditions like Phytophthora root rot, where gummosis and a more significant rotting appearance at the root zone are present.

### Anthracnose Leaf Spot Top Dieback

**Symptoms:**
Anthracnose leaf spot top dieback in corn is characterized by irregular, sunken lesions that range from circular to oval in shape, typically 2-10 mm in diameter, and are found predominantly on the upper leaves. These lesions begin as small, water-soaked spots that progressively turn dark brown to black, surrounded by yellow halos that can be faintly visible, highlighting the tissue discolouration. Affected tissues often exhibit a leathery texture, and as the disease advances, the leaf tips may exhibit wilting, leading to a distinctive dieback. The spores of the fungus may be visible as a fine, black coating on the undersides of leaves, creating a stark contrast against the green foliage. The distribution of symptoms is usually concentrated at the top third of the plant, and the margins of the lesions are often irregular and can appear ragged. Unlike Gray Leaf Spot, which presents as elongated, linear lesions with less pronounced chromatic variation, Anthracnose leaf spot creates more rounded, dark lesions with prominent yellow halos.

While common rust predominantly impacts the leaves, it can occasionally be found on husks and stems, leading to an overall reduction in photosynthetic efficiency. A key difference between common rust and northern corn leaf blight is the presence of these raised, pustule-like lesions in rust, compared to the elongated, canal-shaped lesions characteristic of the latter.

### Common Smut

**Symptoms:**

These galls are generally round to irregular in shape, varying in size from a few centimeters to several inches, and they emerge as raised, swollen structures that can distort the normal form of the corn plant. The galls start off pale green or yellowish and quickly turn to a dark, ash-gray or black color, creating a stark contrast against the healthy green tissue. Upon close inspection, the outer surface of the galls may show a rough texture, and they often burst open, releasing a mass of black spores that cover the surrounding area, providing a distinct coating. The affected areas may also exhibit minimal wilting or yellowing of adjacent tissues, but this is generally mild compared to the galls’ prominence. A key distinguishing feature of common smut is the absence of visible margins or halos around the galls, unlike other diseases such as southern rust, which produce more pronounced lesions with defined borders and are predominantly found on the leaves. Overall, the unique galls and their dark, spore-releasing characteristics set common smut apart from similar diseases in corn.

These symptoms are typically superficial, with a cottony texture that feels velvety to the touch, but there are no raised lesions or distinct margins—rather, the mycelium appears as a fine layer resting upon the leaf surface. The distribution is often random but can become widespread if conditions are favorable, affecting primarily the older leaves while younger leaves remain less impacted. In severe cases, the leaves may exhibit wilting or premature senescence, but no associated structures like exudates or gummosis are usually present. A key difference distinguishing powdery mildew from downy mildew, which also affects mango leaves, is the absence of dark, irregular lesions and the presence of the powdery mycelium on the leaf surface, rather than the fuzzy, downy growth found in downy mildew.

### Sooty Mould

**Symptoms:**
Sooty mould on mango leaves manifests as a distinct superficial black coating that resembles soot, typically covering the upper surfaces of leaves, though it can also appear on stems and fruit. This dark layer obscures the leaf’s natural color and can result in a mottled appearance, particularly in lower leaf canopy areas where moisture is retained. The extent of the sooty mould varies, often developing in patches that can range from small spots to larger, more extensive areas covering entire leaf surfaces. There are no prominent halos or margins around the sooty mould, and it lacks raised or sunken features, as it is a film of fungal spores that does not penetrate the leaf tissue. Unlike similar diseases such as powdery mildew, which presents as white, floury patches and can cause discolouration, sooty mould does not directly damage the leaf tissue but indicates an underlying issue, usually an infestation of sap-sucking pests like aphids or scale insects, leading to honeydew production that fosters the mould growth.

### Anthracnose

**Symptoms:**
Anthracnose stem blight on soybean is characterized by irregularly shaped red to dark brown blotches that appear on stems and petioles during early reproductive stages. Warm, wet weather favors infection and disease development, and crop rotation and tillage can help reduce inoculum levels. A distinguishing feature of anthracnose is the visible fungal bodies that appear late in the season.

### Brown Stem Rot

**Symptoms:**
The visual symptoms of Brown Stem Rot (Cadophora gregata) include chlorosis and necrosis between leaf veins, accompanied by leaf curling and leaf death. The key difference between Brown Stem Rot and Sudden Death Syndrome is that while both can produce similar foliar symptoms, the internal stem browning is a distinguishing feature of Brown Stem Rot, which will not be present in Sudden Death Syndrome.

### Charcoal Rot

**Symptoms:**

### Septoria Brown Spot

**Symptoms:**
Septoria brown spot is the most common foliar disease of soybean, developing soon after planting and typically present throughout the growing season. Symptoms are typically mild during the vegetative stages and progress upward from lower leaves during grain fill. Infected young plants exhibit small, irregularly shaped, dark brown lesions on the unifoliate leaves. Lesions on later leaves are small and found on both leaf surfaces, often growing larger and forming irregularly shaped blotches. Infected leaves quickly turn yellow and drop. The disease starts in the lower canopy, and if conditions remain favorable, it will progress to the upper canopy, potentially leading to yield losses that depend on how far up the canopy the disease progresses during grain fill. 

The key difference between Septoria brown spot and Bacterial blight is that the latter shows tattering of leaf tissues, while Septoria does not.

### Soybean Cyst Nematode

**Symptoms:**
The visual symptoms of the Soybean Cyst Nematode (Heterodera glycines) include noticeable stunting, slow or no canopy closure, and chlorotic foliage. Infected plants display poorly developed root systems and may have reduced numbers of nitrogen-fixing nodules. Symptoms typically occur in patches, and when present, symptomatic plants tend to be stunted. There may be a distinct appearance where infected plants grow slower than non-infected plants, resulting in irregular distributions across the field. Infected roots can show swollen females, which appear as white cysts on the roots. 

A key difference compared to other soybean diseases is that the symptoms of blotching or leaf discoloration do not appear to the same extent as observed in other diseases, such as Sudden Death Syndrome.

### Soybean Dwarf Mosaic Virus

**Symptoms:**
Soybean Dwarf Mosaic Virus manifests primarily on the leaves of soybean plants, exhibiting a characteristic mottling pattern with light green to yellow spots and patches that disrupt the uniformity of the leaf surface. The affected areas may be slightly raised, giving a rough texture in contrast to the surrounding healthy tissue, which remains smooth and dark green. These lesions often appear asymmetrically distributed across the upper leaves and can cause overall leaf distortion and dwarfing of the plant. As the disease progresses, leaf margins may curl downward, showing mottled shades that may resemble a later stage of other viral infections; however, the distinctive yellow mosaic, particularly along veins and the pronounced vein clearing, helps differentiate it. Unlike the symptoms associated with Soybean Mosaic Virus, which tends to produce more uniform chlorotic patterns without the pronounced asymmetrical mottling, Soybean Dwarf Mosaic Virus presents a more chaotic and irregular visual appearance that is crucial for accurate identification in field scouting and automated analysis.

### Soybean Vein Necrosis Virus

**Symptoms:**
Symptoms caused by Soybean vein necrosis virus (SVNV) often begin as yellowing near leaf veins that develops into reddish-brown lesions, causing death of leaf tissue. Lesions follow or spread from leaf veins, and leaf veins may limit the lesions, resulting in an angular lesion appearance. Clear, yellow, or dark brown discoloration of leaf veins can occur, with browning of veins being especially noticeable on the leaf underside. Affected plants are generally scattered randomly in a field, and the distribution of symptoms on a leaf are usually non-uniform. This distinct pattern helps differentiate soybean vein necrosis from visually similar diseases.

### Soybean Rust

**Symptoms:**
Soybean rust (Phakopsora pachyrhizi) symptoms commonly appear on lower leaves after flowering. Initially, lesions form as small gray spots that transition to tan or reddish-brown. These lesions are scattered and appear in yellow areas, becoming translucent when held up to the sun. Mature lesions may contain numerous small pustules, typically found on lower leaf surfaces. The disease is favored by cool, wet conditions or high humidity, and dense canopies provide ideal conditions for its development. The symptoms can be distinguished from similar diseases like bacterial blight and Septoria brown spot by the specific lesion coloration and structure. Compared to Septoria brown spot, Soybean rust tends to show more pronounced yellowing around the lesions.

### Stem Canker

**Symptoms:**
Stem Canker, caused by Diaporthe aspalathi, presents initial symptoms as small, reddish-brown lesions that typically appear near the nodes in the lower third of the soybean canopy after flowering. These lesions expand lengthwise and can form sunken cankers that exhibit a gray-brown color with reddish margins. Mature plants may show irregular black lines, or "zone" lines, on the interior of the lower stems. The disease progression can lead to significant symptoms on infected plants, particularly in susceptible varieties. The progression of stem canker is favored by cooler temperatures and extended periods of wet weather during the early growing season. 

A key difference between Stem Canker and Phytophthora Root and Stem Rot is that the latter is characterized by dark brown lesions that girdle the stem, restricting nutrient flow, whereas Stem Canker presents with reddish-brown sunken lesions and cankers.

### Sudden Death Syndrome

**Symptoms:**
Foliar symptoms of sudden death syndrome (SDS) are caused by a toxin produced by the fungus, moving from roots to the leaves. Initially, leaves of infected plants exhibit scattered yellow spots between leaf veins. These spots develop into larger chlorotic and necrotic blotches, while the midvein and major lateral veins remain green. Eventually, leaflets drop, but petioles remain on the stem. Diseased plants typically have rotted taproots and lateral roots, with the outer tissue of the taproot appearing brown. When stems are split lengthwise, the discoloration of the woody tissue can be light gray to brown and may extend up to two inches above ground. The presence of bluish fungal growth on the roots can be observed if soil moisture is high. Notably, the foliar symptoms of SDS resemble those of brown stem rot, but key differences exist in internal stem browning, as SDS-infected stems maintain white pith, while brown stem rot causes brown discoloration. 

A key difference is that the internal stem condition of plants with sudden death syndrome shows white pith, differing from the brown discoloration characteristic of brown stem rot.

### Tobacco Streak Virus

**Symptoms:**
Tobacco Streak Virus on soybean plants primarily manifests as distinct, angular yellow or light green mosaic patterns on the leaves, with occasional dark brown, necrotic lesions that measure 1-3 mm and can appear sunken or raised. The lesions often have a characteristic halo effect, with their edges sharply defined against the healthy tissue, creating a striking contrast that highlights the surrounding bright green of unaffected foliage. These symptoms can be observed most prominently on the upper leaves; however, they may also spread to the stems, leading to mottling and overall stunting in plant growth. The affected areas may have a rough texture due to the surrounding healthy tissue swelling in response, and in severe cases, galls can develop, particularly on the pods. This viral infection typically does not produce any visible exudates or spores, distinguishing it from diseases like downy mildew, which features fluffy fungal growth. Specifically, the key difference lies in the presence of necrotic lesions without significant fungal growth; this contrasts sharply with downy mildew's distinctive white, cottony mycelium on leaf undersides.

### Top Dieback

**Symptoms:**
Top dieback in soybean plants is characterized by a distinctive pattern of wilting and dieback of the uppermost leaves and stem segments, often exhibiting a progressive yellowing before necrosis occurs. The affected tissues display dark brown to black lesions, which are typically sunken and irregularly shaped, ranging from 1-3 cm in diameter, with a contrasting yellow halo around the edges. These lesions can also form along the stem, leading to discolouration and cankers that give a rough texture compared to healthy tissues. The pods may show stunted growth or premature aging, and occasionally a dark, glossy exudate is evident at the site of infection. In terms of distribution, the symptoms tend to be localized at the top of the plant, creating a clear visual cue of dying tissue amidst greener foliage below. This disease can be distinguished from similar symptoms caused by soybean sudden death syndrome, as the latter typically presents with interveinal chlorosis that progresses to entire leaf wilting without the distinct dark lesions characteristic of top dieback.

### White Mold

**Symptoms:**
White mold, also known as Sclerotinia Stem Rot, is characterized by fluffy, white growth on soybean stems. Initial symptoms generally develop from R3 to R6 as gray to white lesions at the nodes. These lesions rapidly progress above and below the nodes, sometimes girdling the stem. The white, fluffy mold soon covers the infected area, particularly during periods of high relative humidity. Initial foliar symptoms include leaf tissues between major veins turning a gray-green cast; eventually, leaves die and turn completely brown while remaining attached to the stem. Characteristic black sclerotia eventually become visible, embedded within the mold on stem lesions and inside the stem as the plant approaches death. 

A key difference between white mold and other stem diseases is the distinct presence of the white, fluffy growth and subsequent sclerotia formation.

## Crop: Wheat Diseases

### Rust

**Symptoms:**
Rust on wheat is characterized by distinct, bright orange to reddish-brown pustules that appear mainly on the upper surface of leaves, although they can also be observed on stems and, less commonly, on the ears. These pustules are raised, often surrounded by a faint yellow halo, and may vary in size from 1 to 5 mm, exhibiting a clustered distribution that can lead to significant defoliation in severe cases. The affected tissue displays a rough texture due to the clusters of spores, which can erupt and release powdery spores when disturbed, giving a dusty appearance. As the disease progresses, the leaves may develop areas of necrosis, leading to wilting and premature senescence. Unlike other foliar diseases such as powdery mildew, which produces a white dusty coating and primarily affects the upper leaf surface with a more uniform distribution, wheat rust is specifically notable for its raised pustules and vibrant coloration that distinctly contrast against the green foliage.
